How I build an app, step by step

The eight steps I follow to take an app from idea to the App Store, and what you get from each one.

Nishanth Kumar 26 November 2026 4 min read

Founders often ask me what building an app together actually looks like. This is the process I follow, whether I’m building for a client or with a founder as a co-founder. It’s the same eight steps every time, and each one ends with something concrete in your hands.

1. Discovery call

We talk through the idea, who it’s for and what success looks like. I ask a lot of questions about the users and the problem, and fewer about features. I’ll also be honest if I think something isn’t worth building yet.

You get: a clear view of whether, and how, to build it.

2. Scope the first version

Most ideas start too big. Together we cut it down to the smallest version worth putting in people’s hands, and write down what comes later so nothing is lost.

You get: a feature list for version one, a timeline and an estimate.

3. Design

Design happens in Figma, with your designer or one we bring in. I review every screen while it’s still cheap to change, checking that it’s practical to build and pleasant to use on a real phone.

You get: clickable designs for the key flows.

4. Architecture and setup

Here I choose how to build it: native Swift and SwiftUI, React Native with TypeScript, or a mix. I set up the project, the backend and automated builds, so every change is tested and shipped the same way from day one.

You get: a codebase a future team can pick up and work in.

5. Build in short cycles

I build in one- or two-week cycles. At the end of each, you get a new build through TestFlight, so you see progress on your own phone instead of in status reports. Feedback goes straight into the next cycle.

You get: a working app that improves every week.

6. Test

Logic is covered by automated tests with XCTest, and key flows like sign-up or checkout get UI tests with XCUITest. I also test on real devices, different screen sizes and older iOS versions, because that’s where surprises hide.

You get: fewer surprises at launch.

7. Launch

Launch is more than pressing a button. It means App Store and Google Play listings, screenshots, privacy details and getting through review. I’ve shipped apps to both stores many times, so I know what reviewers look for.

You get: your app live in the stores.

8. Improve and grow

After launch, crash reports, analytics and user feedback decide what comes next. If you’re hiring your own team, I help onboard them and hand over a codebase they can move fast in.

You get: a product that keeps getting better.

Native, cross-platform or both?

Step four is where this gets decided. I go native with Swift and SwiftUI when the app lives mostly on iPhone and iPad, needs the best performance and animation, or depends on new Apple features. I reach for React Native with TypeScript when you need iOS and Android from day one with a small team, or your team already works in React. I wrote more about that choice in Why I build React Native apps with TypeScript.

What makes it work

None of these steps is unusual on its own. What makes the difference is keeping them small and visible: a scope you can read in five minutes, a build on your phone every week, and honest conversations whenever something takes longer than planned.
